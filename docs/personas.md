# Two working personas for content and journeys

Depth pass 2026-10-31. These remain design hypotheses built from desk research,
not interview findings — validate with real readers before treating them as
evidence. Sources below are public reference material checked on 2026-10-31.

## Evidence base

- **CISCRP (Center for Information and Study on Clinical Research Participation)**
  runs the largest ongoing patient/care-partner perceptions research
  (https://www.ciscrp.org/, "Perceptions and Insights Study"). Repeated findings:
  participation decisions weigh **altruism ("help people like me") and personal
  health benefit together**; the biggest barriers are **fear of side effects /
  placebo / randomization, logistical burden (travel, time, cost), and lack of
  trust**; the single strongest trigger to consider a trial is **a recommendation
  from one's own doctor or nurse**; and **care partners do much of the searching
  and reading**, often before the patient sees the page.
- **NIA clinical-trials guidance** (https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/clinical-trials)
  frames participant decisions around "benefits, risks, and safety",
  "what to ask before participating", informed consent as a process, and why
  older/diverse participants are specifically needed.
- **NIHR Be Part of Research** organizes everything around participant questions:
  "Who can take part", "What to expect on a study", "Consenting to a study" —
  plain status words, dated listings, no source bookkeeping in the UI.
- **Older-adult participation literature** (trial-barrier reviews, e.g. work
  summarised in JCO/SRCT analyses of participation demographics): older adults
  weigh visit/test burden, transport help from family, medication interactions,
  and the chance of closer medical monitoring; comorbidity and pill burden make
  "will this interact with my medicines?" a first-order question.
- **Clinician referral research**: referrers engage only if relevance is obvious
  in under two minutes, criteria + sites + burden are stated, and the referral
  step is one secure action. Promotional tone, undated status, or implying
  patient details can be emailed are immediate drop-offs.
- Decision frameworks used here: the **Common-Sense Model** (people build an
  illness meaning and check new information against it) and the **Ottawa Decision
  Support Framework** (decision needs: clarify the choice, resolve values, take
  action) — both point to relevance-first copy and explicit "what happens next".

## Persona A — "Dr. Maya", clinician-referrer and potential collaborator

- **Context:** mid-career hospital diabetologist / clinical researcher, ~45.
  Runs a busy older-adult diabetes clinic (15+ patients a day), supervises
  students, publishes. Hebrew-first, reads papers in English. Browses on a
  desktop between clinics; forwards links by WhatsApp or prints them for patients.
- **Two hats, one visit:** (1) "is there a study for the complex patient I saw
  this morning?" (2) "is this a group I could work with / send a student to?"
  She wears hat 1 far more often but judges credibility by hat 2's standards.
- **Goals:** in ≤2 minutes determine who a study is for, what it asks of a
  patient, where and when it runs — and find one trustworthy, shareable link;
  for collaboration: methods, real publications (DOI), named people, current work.
- **Behaviors:** scans status **and its date** first; skims "who it's for" and
  "what's involved"; opens the official registry record to verify; reads
  publications by title and journal; abandons pages that sound promotional,
  undated, or written like a patient leaflet _on professional pages_.
- **Motivations:** evidence-based options for patients who fall between
  guidelines; being a good referral partner so specialists send patients back;
  publications, methods she can borrow, supervision fit for students.
- **Fears/friction:** an outdated "recruiting" badge ruining her credibility with
  a patient; no clear referral channel (where would patient details go?); jargon
  on patient pages she'd have to re-explain; email-only contact for referrals;
  hidden costs or unclear eligibility making referrals look sloppy.
- **Needs per page:** dated plain status; eligibility summary + link to full
  criteria; sites; visit/time burden; a privacy-safe "how to refer" step; on
  research pages — methods vocabulary, links to studies and publications, the
  team's names and roles; stable bilingual deep links she can share.
- **Journey:** trigger (a patient / a colleague / a paper) → Studies list scan →
  study page → verify official record → share link or follow referral route →
  (second visit) Research → Publications → collaboration contact.
- **Working quote (hypothesis):** "If I can't tell in two minutes who this is for
  and what it would ask of my patient, it's easier not to refer."

## Persona B — "Rivka", 72 — and her son "Dan" who helps her look

- **Context:** Rivka has type 2 diabetes, arthritis, and a pillbox; she reads on
  an Android phone with large text, Hebrew-first, sometimes tired in the
  evenings. Dan, 44, searches on a laptop and sends her WhatsApp links. Neither
  has ever joined research; both know someone who "was in a study".
- **Two readers, one decision:** Dan screens first ("is this real, is it
  relevant, is it safe"), then reads the practical parts aloud to Rivka. Rivka
  decides. Pages must survive both readings.
- **Goals:** understand what the study is about **for someone like her**; what it
  would actually require (getting to Sheba/Hadassah, tests, exercise, injections,
  how many times); whether it is safe _with her other medicines_; whether it
  costs money; whether she can stop.
- **Behaviors:** headline → "who is it for" → "what's involved" → asks Dan to
  check "if it's legitimate"; trusts hospital/university names and her own
  doctor's word most; suspicious of anything that feels like an ad or asks for
  medical details online; wants days, not minutes, to decide.
- **Motivations:** helping future patients "so others have it easier"; closer
  attention to her own health; doing something meaningful; possible personal
  benefit — which must never be promised to her.
- **Fears:** being an experiment ("guinea pig"), placebos, side effects on top of
  her existing medicines, the burden on Dan for rides, losing control, scams
  asking for money or documents.
- **Decision stages the site must support:** (1) _noticing_ — a plain headline
  that names the condition; (2) _personal relevance_ — "who it is for" in human
  words, early; (3) _sense-making_ — visits, tests, required vs optional, risks,
  costs, voluntariness; (4) _discussion_ — something concrete to take to family
  and her regular doctor; (5) _making contact_ — knowing that asking is a
  question, not a commitment, and what will happen after.
- **Needs per page:** short sentences; technical terms explained once; time and
  travel commitment stated honestly; required vs optional procedures (e.g. the
  optional muscle biopsy) never buried; "you can leave at any time"; no forms
  collecting health details; a dated status so she knows the page is alive;
  large tap targets and no horizontal scrolling on her phone.
- **Working quote (hypothesis):** "Dan, will they want money — and can I stop if
  it becomes too much?"

## Cross-persona rules (from the evidence above)

1. **Relevance before detail.** Every study page answers, in order: what is this
   → who is it for → what would it involve → what to consider → what happens
   next → where to verify. (Common-Sense relevance check; NIA "what to ask".)
2. **Status is plain and dated.** "Recruiting" + "Last updated 21 July 2026";
   provenance stays in quiet source lines. (NIHR pattern.)
3. **Ask = question.** Any contact language says it is not joining, not consent,
   and that the study team decides eligibility. (CISCRP: fear of commitment.)
4. **Doctor-in-the-loop cues.** Encourage discussing with family and one's own
   physician — physician recommendation is the decisive trigger for both
   personas. (CISCRP.)
5. **Two tones, same facts.** Patient paths: plain Hebrew/English, burden and
   safety first. Clinician paths: precise terms with one-line glosses, sources,
   one-step referral. Never patient cheerleading on professional pages.
6. **Care-partner legibility.** Pages must make sense when read aloud and when
   forwarded as a link; sentences short, headings literal, phones supported.
7. **Build-state copy is not user copy.** Lines like "Enquiries are not enabled
   on this draft" are scaffolding for the owner and disappear at launch — do not
   polish them into the voice of the site.

## Site changes this depth pass drove

- Study pages gain a "Things to consider" list (voluntariness, family/doctor
  discussion, eligibility by the team, asking ≠ joining) beside the contact
  state — the decision-support content Rivka and Dan look for.
- Enquiry-unavailable copy simplified to a terse build-state note (rule 7).
- Earlier label work ("Who the study is for", "What taking part involves", plain
  dated status) already implements rules 1–3; further page-level copy should be
  checked against rules 4–6 when the study pages are revised.
